# IAA_CSC_Climate_Report_2026_09.pdf

## Executive Summary

The most consequential development of this cycle is that the official temperature baseline moved: UNEP's
Limiting Overshoot, published 2 September, is its first formal acknowledgement that exceeding 1.5  °C is
unavoidable, and reframes the objective as overshoot, peak and decline. Alongside it, the WMO hardened its El
Niño call to a near-certainty of persistence through February 2027 at "very strong" intensity — the first such
unequivocal statement in its fifty-year history — against a record-warm August. A third strand is quietly more
actionable for actuaries: three separate bodies moved climate exposure from assertion to measurement, with the
WMO reporting that conventional models may understate wildfire-attributable mortality by up to 93%, the OECD
publishing a new spatial general equilibrium model of macroeconomic climate damage, and the World Bank
issuing a cost-effectiveness evidence base for health-sector adaptation. Meanwhile sovereign parametric cover
visibly performed, with live payouts in Laos and Hawaii, even as UNCCD COP17 closed having deferred a global
drought framework to COP18.

MOST SIGNIFICANT DEVELOPMENTS
UNEP abandons the hold-below-1.5 °C frame
Limiting Overshoot (2 September) assesses exceedance as unavoidable and sets an "overshoot, peak
and decline" trajectory, with a best case peaking near 1.8  °C and other pathways above 2  °C. The
accompanying explainer makes a point that matters more than the headline: planetary systems respond
slowly and unpredictably, so an overshoot-and-return temperature path does not imply an overshoot-
and-return impact path. Hazard frequencies should not be modelled as reversing symmetrically with
temperature, and the central case for actuarial scenario work should now carry an overshoot shape.
1
WMO hardens the El Niño call to near-certainty
The 3 September update gives an "exceptionally high likelihood of nearly 100%" of El Niño persisting
through February 2027, peaking at very strong intensity around end-2026, with the SON Niño 3.4
anomaly forecast near +3.6  °C and a positive Indian Ocean Dipole alongside. August 2026 was
simultaneously the hottest August on record at 1.65 °C above pre-industrial, with a record daily sea-
surface temperature of 21.11 °C and 19 tropical storms. This is a high-confidence, multi-quarter shift in
the hazard baseline spanning two treaty years.
2
Wildfire smoke mortality may be understated by up to 93%
WMO Air Quality and Climate Bulletin No. 6 (7 September) finds extreme fire-smoke events have tripled
globally since the 1990s, contributing roughly 100,000 additional deaths annually over 2010–2018, and
that conventional risk models may understate wildfire-attributable mortality by as much as 93%. This is
a direct challenge to mortality and morbidity assumptions in health, life and critical-illness pricing, and
establishes wildfire smoke as a non-property loss channel in its own right.
3
A new top-down damage benchmark from the OECD
OECD-CASGEM (3 September) links location-specific hazards to economy-wide outcomes through
trade, migration and production networks, giving global GDP per capita losses of 3% by 2050 and 6.3%
by 2100 under SSP2-4.5, rising to 6% and 18% under SSP5-8.5. Its finding that spatial adjustment acts
as "implicit loss-sharing" across regions is conceptually important for anyone modelling correlated
global damages, and the figures are usable as a calibration benchmark for climate scenario analysis.
4
Parametric cover visibly performed
SEADRIF paid over US$1m to the Lao PDR sovereign and the WFP; the Nature Conservancy's Hawaii
parametric coral reef policy triggered for the first time ever; the EIB and WFP launched a smallholder
scheme in Ethiopia; and ADB deployed its new region-wide contingent facility (3RDO) for the first time
in Vanuatu, a country losing an average US$35.7m a year to disasters, 86% of it weather-related. Live
payouts are the best available evidence on whether these triggers actually perform and on realised
basis risk.
5
A compound cross-border cryosphere event in Nepal
An ice-and-rock avalanche on the Tibet border on 26 August triggered a flash flood in which the Trishuli
rose nine metres in thirty minutes. The WMO confirmed 359 deaths by 27 August; the FAO
subsequently reported over 1,000 deaths, roughly 1,800 hectares of mostly paddy destroyed, nearly
7,700 tonnes of production lost and 41 bridges destroyed. Cascading glacial-lake and avalanche-
triggered surge events of this kind sit poorly in conventional flood catastrophe models — and the World
Bank's Nepal parametric earthquake cat bond remains unplaced.
6
UNCCD COP17 closed short on drought
The conference produced a US$1.3bn land restoration and drought resilience portfolio, of which
US$644.5m is new money, against an estimated US$278bn annual financing gap — and countries
failed to agree a global drought framework, deferring it to COP18. The AfDB separately committed
US$500m over 2027–2031 for Zambezi Basin restoration. The scale of the shortfall is the clearest
available quantification of the drought protection gap.

CROSS-CUTTING THEMES
1. The baseline moved, and asymmetrically. UNEP's overshoot framing, echoed within days by the WEF
and WRI, changes the default shape of a climate scenario. The subtlety worth carrying into technical work
is that impacts are not expected to retrace the temperature path — tipping-point risk accumulates during
exceedance and does not unwind on the way back down.
2. A high-confidence hazard signal for two treaty years. The El Niño call, the record August, the FAO
Food Price Index attribution to heat and drought in the EU and to El Niño in palm oil and Asian rice, and the
Regulatory divergence now quantified — and the G20 confirmed out
UNCTAD's Sustainable Finance Policy Monitor 2026 (8 September) counts 143 new sustainable
finance policies across 35 economies in 2025, up from 99 in 2024, for 804 cumulative measures, with
disclosure at 44% of new policies and increasingly ISSB-aligned, plus 18 new carbon pricing policies.
Against that, the G20 Chair's Statement from Asheville (1 September) lists seven US presidency
finance-track priorities with no mention of climate, sustainable finance or transition, and the WRI
documents a US retreat from carbon dioxide removal support while the EU, Canada, Germany and the
UK advance.
8
Health-sector adaptation acquires a cost-effectiveness evidence base
The World Bank's Smart Buys: High-Value Actions for Health Sector Adaptation (3 September) ranks
adaptation interventions by value, published as a three-part package. The WHO added a 63-page
global public health situation analysis for El Niño 2026 (5 September) and a South-East Asia climate
and health regional profile (4 September). Together these give health actuaries a hazard-exposure layer
and an intervention cost-effectiveness ranking that did not previously exist in usable form.
9
WRI's Super El Niño explainer all point the same way. Unusually, this is a forward signal with enough
confidence to act on rather than a retrospective attribution.
3. Climate exposure is being converted into measured mortality. Wildfire smoke (WMO), European
heat deaths above 200,000 over four years (WEF citing WHO), El Niño public health analysis (WHO) and
health-adaptation cost-effectiveness (World Bank) together mark a shift from qualitative concern to
quantified, priceable exposure in health and life portfolios.
4. Risk transfer is proving itself while the protection gap widens. Parametric instruments paid out in
Laos and Hawaii and a contingent sovereign facility deployed in Vanuatu — at the same time as COP17 left
the drought framework unagreed and a US$278bn annual gap unaddressed. The instruments work; the
coverage does not yet reach the exposure.
5. Jurisdictional divergence is now the dominant transition-risk variable. Disclosure and carbon
pricing regimes are proliferating and converging on the ISSB outside the United States, while US federal
support retreats and the G20 channel has closed. Expect the quality and comparability of climate risk
inputs to depend increasingly on where the exposure sits.
NOTABLY QUIET
The prudential bodies produced no climate output at all across three weeks, and this was established by direct
enumeration rather than inference: BIS working papers, bulletins and FSI Insights were each probed and
opened, and all in-window items concern reserve demand, supervisory language models, small-bank
proportionality, stablecoins and AI cyber threats; BCBS publication numbering confirms nothing since 2 June; the
FSB published five items, of which the Chair's August G20 letter and an operational-resilience roundtable were
opened and confirmed to contain no climate content; NGFS published nothing; and every IMF working paper
from No. 177 to No. 191 was enumerated and confirmed non-climate. On the insurance side the Geneva
Association, FINZ, IRFF and PRI were all silent, as were PCAF, ISSB, IFAC and TCFD. The pattern is
consistent: the sector is holding its output for the Dublin summit and Climate Week, both of which fall in the next
reporting period.
