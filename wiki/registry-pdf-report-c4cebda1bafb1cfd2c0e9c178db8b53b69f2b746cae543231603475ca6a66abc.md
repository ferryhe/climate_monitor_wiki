## Executive Summary · 采用原文

Provenance: adopted original PDF text; independent factual verification is not asserted.

Three developments dominate this fortnight. The World Trade Organization's Dispute Settlement Body established
a panel on 25 September to adjudicate the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, the first
time a carbon border measure will be tested in WTO dispute settlement and a conversion of CBAM from a settled
scenario parameter into a contested one. The NGFS published the first full rewrite of its supervisory guide since
2020, putting nature-related risk on the same prudential footing as climate and, on secondary reporting,
contemplating capital buffers against climate-vulnerable regional concentration and explicit supervisory monitoring
of the insurance protection gap — protection-gap metrics migrating from insurance supervision into bank capital-
setting. Against those, the 2026 G20 presidency has run its Energy Abundance Ministerial to a communiqué with
no mention of climate change, decarbonisation or climate finance, and operates no Sustainable Finance, climate
or disaster risk reduction working group at all. The emerging theme across all three is divergence: the supervisory
and scientific machinery is deepening while the political machinery thins, which widens rather than narrows the
range of defensible transition scenarios. Beneath them, the fortnight was unusually rich in quantified physical-risk
material — WMO's State of Global Water Resources 2025, Swiss Re's 5–7% structural loss-trend estimate, and
FAO's USD 566 billion African adaptation costing.

MOST SIGNIFICANT DEVELOPMENTS
WTO establishes a dispute panel on the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism
On 25 September the Dispute Settlement Body agreed to the Russian Federation's request for a panel
to review the EU CBAM package and an alleged export subsidy under the EU emissions allowance
trading scheme, the request having been blocked once at the 24 July DSB meeting. This is the first
adjudication of a carbon border measure under WTO dispute settlement. For scenario work it means
the carbon-price and border-adjustment assumptions embedded in EU-exposed industrial transition
scenarios now carry genuine legal uncertainty; for liability work it is the clearest instance yet of climate
policy generating first-order international dispute risk. No panel timetable has been published.
1
NGFS rewrites its supervisory guide and brings nature onto the same footing as climate
The first full rewrite since May 2020 adds nature-related financial risk, transition planning, adaptation,
emerging stress-testing practice, litigation risk and disclosure, evidenced by a member survey covering
70% of NGFS jurisdictions. Secondary reporting states the guide contemplates capital buffers for banks
with concentrated exposure to climate-vulnerable regions or sectors, additional capital against large
mortgage books in flood-prone areas, loan-to-value adjustments for green assets, and explicit
supervisory monitoring of the insurance protection gap for financial-stability purposes. Those specifics
are secondary-sourced and should be confirmed against the primary text, but the direction is
unambiguous: nat-cat concentration and protection-gap metrics are becoming bank prudential inputs.
2
The G20 climate and sustainable finance workstream has been stood down for 2026
The 2026 United States presidency runs four working groups — Trade, Growth and Deregulation,
Innovation, and Energy Abundance. There is no Sustainable Finance Working Group, no climate or
environment working group and no disaster risk reduction working group. The Energy Abundance
Ministerial Chair's Statement of 16 September contains no reference to climate change,
decarbonisation targets or climate finance; its only emissions language is a neutral list item. For
transition-risk purposes the absence is the signal, and it argues for reweighting probability away from
rapid-transition pathways and towards delayed-and-disorderly and hot-house scenarios in near-term
stress testing.
3
NGFS Phase V chronic physical-risk estimates are being replaced after the underlying
damage function was retracted
NGFS has committed to a new chronic physical-risk methodology for its long-term scenarios in late
2026, overseen by a newly established independent scientific advisory committee, following the
retraction from Nature of the Kotz et al. damage function that underpins the Phase V estimates. Short-
term scenarios are unaffected. This is the highest-priority forward item on the calendar for any firm that
has used Phase V chronic physical-loss estimates in ORSA, ICAAP or public climate disclosure: a
material recalibration should be planned for rather than absorbed when it lands.
4
Cat bond risk-adjusted returns have deteriorated sharply while the market sets issuance
records
AM Best reports cat bond loss multiples compressing to 2.5x in H1 2026 from 3.28x in H1 2025, with
the 2026 year-to-date figure at 2.4x against 3.71x in 2024, while weighted average spread fell 96 basis
points and expected loss on new issuance rose 30 basis points. Simultaneously, alternative capital
reached a record USD 147 billion in H1 2026, H1 issuance a record USD 18 billion, and outstanding
market size USD 65.6 billion at end-Q2. Moody's expects property reinsurance prices down 7.5–15% in
2027 and Fitch finds 86% expect terms and conditions to loosen. Cheaper climate capacity is available,
on visibly worse risk-adjusted terms and with erosion concentrated in attachment points and hours
clauses that rate indices do not capture.
5
WMO finds the global water cycle has lost stationarity, not merely shifted
The State of Global Water Resources 2025 records 2025 among the driest years for river discharge in
over 35 years, with below-normal flows across 36% of global basin area and, for a seventh consecutive
year, basins in normal condition a minority at 34–38% against a 1991–2020 norm of 46%. Glaciers lost
1,400 gigatonnes over 2023–2025; nearly two-thirds of monitored groundwater wells sat outside
historical norms. The finding that matters technically is the oscillation between too little and too much
rather than a directional trend, which degrades the stationarity assumptions underlying flood and
drought models more than a simple shift in the mean would.
6
The Bermuda Monetary Authority will regulate parametric risk transfer on substance, not
contractual form
Responding to feedback on its January 2026 consultation, the BMA confirmed that its new Parametric
Special Purpose Insurer class will admit contracts structured as reinsurance, derivatives or swaps
provided statutory requirements are met, that existing SPIs may transition, and that some may write
both indemnity and parametric business case by case. An amendment to the Insurance Act 1978 is
being drafted for Q4 2026. This creates a purpose-built collateralised vehicle for parametric climate risk
transfer in the market's principal domicile and removes a long-standing structuring constraint.
7
TNFD-aligned reporting doubled year on year as the ISSB nature exposure draft approaches
The TNFD 2026 Status Report records over 1,000 organisations across 56 countries publishing TNFD-
aligned disclosures, a 100% year-on-year increase, with 802 committed Adopters and USD 26.6 trillion
of AUM represented by financial-institution adopters. Every investor surveyed reported concern about
nature loss affecting financial markets and over 70% supported mandatory nature-related reporting.
Read alongside the NGFS guide's nature content and the ISSB exposure draft expected in October
ahead of CBD COP17, nature risk is converging on the same supervisory and reporting architecture as
climate on a compressed timetable.
8
Adaptation finance gained both a measurement argument and a costed denominator
WEF published the clearest mainstream statement yet that the measurement barrier to adaptation
finance has been cleared — climate analytics now support credible avoided-loss quantification —
against mitigation still taking over 90% of climate finance and only 9% of assessed companies
committing capital to adaptation. FAO supplied the denominator: 46 of 53 African countries have costed
combined adaptation needs of USD 566 billion, about USD 56 billion a year, of which at least USD 80
billion is agricultural. ADB added the mechanism, with a briefing on donor-funded premium subsidy
design for Pacific risk pools. Together these are the components of an avoided-loss metric for
adaptation underwriting, which is squarely actuarial territory and currently unclaimed.

⚠  TIME-SENSITIVE ITEMS REQUIRING ATTENTION
1. ISSB Sustainability Disclosure Taxonomy — GHG amendments consultation closes today, 28
September 2026. This governs the machine-readable tagging of amended IFRS S2 greenhouse gas
disclosures, and therefore how financed-emissions and Scope 3 data reach insurers' and asset owners'
transition-risk analytics. If the Committee intends to respond, today is the last opportunity.
2. IPCC AR7 Working Group I First Order Draft expert review closes 2 October 2026. Reviewer
registration closed on 25 September, inside this window, so the pool is now fixed: actuaries not already
registered cannot comment on the physical science basis that will set the next generation of hazard
assumptions. Members who did register have four days left to file comments.
3. NGFS long-term climate scenarios will be recalibrated in late 2026. The chronic physical-risk
methodology is being rebuilt after the retraction of the underlying damage function. Firms using Phase V
chronic physical-loss estimates in ORSA, ICAAP or disclosure should identify that dependency now rather
than after publication. Short-term scenarios are unaffected.
4. The PSI white paper From Vulnerability to Insurability launches 13–14 November 2026 at the COP31
Global Sustainable Insurance Summit in Antalya. This is the UN insurance body's formal position on
making climate-vulnerable assets insurable, and the single most actuarially material scheduled publication
on the forward calendar. Worth diarising now, alongside the IAIS Annual Conference on 12–13 November,
whose announced themes include nat-cat resilience.
5. The ISSB nature-related exposure draft is expected in October with a 120-day comment period. All
twelve ISSB members approved the ballot on 21 July 2026 and the release is timed to precede CBD COP17
(19–30 October). No date has been published, so this needs active watching rather than diary entry.
CROSS-CUTTING THEMES
1. Supervisory deepening against political thinning. The NGFS supervisory guide, the ISSB nature
exposure draft and the TNFD adoption evidence base all advanced this fortnight, while the G20 abolished
its sustainable finance workstream and the US EPA repealed carbon limits on power plants. These are not
contradictory signals to be averaged; they are a widening of the distribution. Scenario sets built on a single
central transition pathway are becoming harder to defend than they were a year ago.
2. Nature risk is converging on the climate architecture, fast. NGFS placed nature alongside climate in
prudential supervision, TNFD reported adoption doubling to over 1,000 organisations and issued its
eighteenth sector guidance, TNFD and UNEP FI published a bank chief risk officer guide, the World Bank
assessed nature-based solutions for disaster risk reduction in Central Asia, and the ISSB exposure draft is
weeks away. Firms that treated nature as a later problem now have a compressed runway.
3. Physical risk is being reframed as correlated multi-system failure. WMO documented loss of
stationarity in the water cycle; WEF's heat coverage showed grid, water and health systems failing
together; the Nepal–Tibet flood originated outside the affected country's monitoring network entirely; CatIQ
found flooding displacing hail and wind as the dominant Prairie peril. Each is an argument against treating
perils as independent draws, and for accumulation and compound-event scenario design.
4. The data layer beneath the models is thinner than the models imply. WMO's iClimateAction reports
found in-situ networks for the 55 Essential Climate Variables dependent on short-term projects and
volunteers with material gaps in Africa, South America, Southeast Asia and the polar regions; UNDP put
LiDAR coverage of vulnerable atoll nations at 15.6% of land area; OECD identified community-level
exposure data as the binding constraint on micro-disaster risk finance; and WMO flagged radio spectrum
loss as a threat to forecast skill. This is parameter uncertainty that is rarely priced and almost never
disclosed.
5. Cheaper catastrophe capacity on worse terms. Compressing cat bond multiples, rising expected loss
on new issuance, expected 2027 property reinsurance rate falls of 7.5–15%, 86% expecting terms and
conditions to loosen, and a 50% rebound in traded aggregate excess-of-loss limits describe a market in
which climate-driven capacity is becoming cheaper and structurally looser at the same time. The erosion is
concentrated in attachment points and hours clauses, which rate indices do not show.
6. Adaptation is acquiring a price, and the metric is unclaimed. WEF on avoided-loss measurement,
FAO's USD 566 billion African costing, ADB's premium-subsidy and investment-planning work, GCA's nine
project exemplars and WRI's 10:1 adaptation benefit-cost ratio all point at the same missing object: an
agreed method for valuing avoided climate loss. That is the same quantity a catastrophe model produces,
and the profession is better placed to define it than anyone currently attempting to.
NOTABLY QUIET
Fourteen monitored organisations were checked on a working route and confirmed to have published nothing in
scope. The financial-regulatory cluster was almost entirely silent: BIS published six items in the window, none
climate, its most recent climate output being a central bankers' speech of 7 May 2026; BCBS published only the
Basel III Monitoring Report and has issued no climate publication since April 2024; the FSB published nothing at
all between 14 and 27 September. IAIS was silent across news, press releases, publications and its own climate
tag, with its September newsletter still unissued at the time of this run and no open consultations — a notable
gap given that nat-cat resilience is an announced theme of its November Annual Conference. ISSB was
confirmed quiet this cycle via a browser session that rendered the JavaScript-driven IFRS news hub, closing a
gap that has been open since the first edition. The Geneva Association published one report in the window, on
financial fragmentation, which was opened and assessed as carrying no climate, transition or nat-cat content.
WHO produced no climate-and-health item at headquarters or in the European region, its most recent being 16
July 2026. Also confirmed quiet: IPCC (whose year index shows nothing after 6 July), IFAC, ILO, SIF,
CarbonPool, IRFF and TCFD, the last of which has been an archived site since November 2023 and is
recommended for reduction to an annual spot-check.
PCAF deserves separate mention: it held a New York Climate Week session on 21 September but published no
technical output, consultation or methodology release during the fortnight, and its insurance-associated
emissions consultation page remains closed with no dates shown. Given that two of its seven 2026–2028 priority
topics are insurance methodologies, the silence is itself worth tracking.

duplicates were found; every item below is new to this series. Of the 42 monitored organisations, 21 reported

roughly twelve days, that content is now permanently lost to this series. Items where verification was partial — a
date confirmed only to the month, a figure taken from secondary reporting, a landing page that rendered no
abstract — are flagged in place rather than silently upgraded. Appendix A records the basis for every one of the
42; Appendix B records what broke and what was fixed.
