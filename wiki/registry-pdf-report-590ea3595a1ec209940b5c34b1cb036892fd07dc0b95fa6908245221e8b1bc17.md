## Executive Summary

A strengthening El Niño became the defining climate signal of the fortnight, with the WMO, FAO, ILO and WFP each
independently warning of compounding food-security, health and labour-market consequences into 2027, against a July
that tied 2024 as the joint-warmest on record. UNCCD COP17 in Ulaanbaatar (17–28 August) served as the organising
event of the period, pulling the African Development Bank, the Asian Development Bank, UNEP and UN-Water into a
coordinated push on drought resilience and land-degradation finance, and producing several new blended-finance
vehicles. The most directly actuarial contribution came from the ILO, which published the first jurisdiction-wide
quantification of occupational heat mortality in Colombia — usable excess-death rates by occupation and temperature
band. Meanwhile the disclosure architecture continued to consolidate around the ISSB and PCAF even as the United
States moved in the opposite direction, with the G20's 2026 US presidency removing climate and sustainable finance from
its agenda entirely.

MOST SIGNIFICANT DEVELOPMENTS
Quantified occupational heat mortality (ILO)
Working Paper 178 finds excess mortality of 0.09 deaths per 1,000 workers above 32 °C in Colombia, rising to
1.33 for assemblers and 0.40 for health associate professionals, with only 53% of the workforce holding
occupational-risk cover. These are directly applicable loadings for life, disability and workers' compensation
pricing, and the clearest example yet of climate exposure translated into insurable mortality parameters.

Strong El Niño converging across four institutions
WMO's Greater Horn of Africa outlook, the FAO-led State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2026, an
ILO transmission-channel brief for Latin America and the Caribbean, and WFP's estimate of ~49 million
additional people in acute food insecurity by end-2027 all landed within eight days. Treat this as one coordinated
early-action signal rather than four items.
2
Drought and land-degradation finance scaled at COP17
ADB launched the FIELD initiative (at least US$2bn by 2035 via derisking structures and capital-market
instruments) and the GIRAF fund for Mongolia (US$92.6m Green Climate Fund plus ~US$25m commercial
capital, using credit guarantees and remote-sensing monitoring); AfDB launched a Drought Resilience
Investment Facility; UNEP proposed a Drought Resilience Capacity Index. The WEF costs land degradation at
US$878bn annually.
3
A carbon-cycle feedback missing from standard scenarios
WEF reports that circumpolar wildfire plus post-fire permafrost thaw could release 147–275 GtCO2e by 2100,
with 335 Mha burned globally in 2025 — a feedback largely absent from NGFS-style scenario sets that actuaries
rely on for transition and physical pathways.

Physical-risk data improving, US disclosure data degrading
WRI's Global Forest Watch update shows fires accounting for ~43% of tree-cover loss in 2023–2025 against a
27% long-run average, with 1.5m+ annual deaths attributed to wildfire smoke. In parallel, WRI warns that
rescinding the US SEC climate disclosure rule would remove standardised physical- and transition-risk data
backed by 320 institutional investors managing US$50tn.
5
Disclosure convergence continues
EFRAG joined PCAF's Engagement Group for Central Banks and Financial Regulators to align ESRS with
financed-emissions methodology, and the ISSB has resolved to ballot an exposure draft of a nature-related
Practice Statement for October 2026 publication — the TNFD framework migrating into the ISSB architecture.
6
Insurance substituting for over-collateralisation in carbon markets
CarbonPool has secured Verra approval for reversal insurance under the Durability Pilot, allowing projects with
2026–2028 issuances to replace the standard 10–30% buffer-pool contribution with an insurance premium, with
claims paid in carbon credits. This is the carbon market adopting actuarial machinery in place of crude reserving.
7
A Southwest Indian Ocean adaptation cluster from the World Bank
Paired fisheries-adaptation and blue-economy analyses for Madagascar and the Comoros, Pacific Islands
energy resilience roadmaps, and natural capital accounting roadmaps for Ethiopia and South Africa all disclosed
within ten days — alongside a practitioner handbook on embedding hazard and risk information into urban
planning that is the most directly transferable item of the cycle for property and urban exposure work.
8
The G20 climate workstream has lost its sponsor
The 2026 US presidency makes no reference to climate or sustainable finance in its published agenda, and the
Sustainable Finance Working Group shows no 2026 activity. The G20 Sustainable Finance Roadmap, a
reference point for several supervisory climate workstreams, is effectively dormant this year.

2. A "triple COP" year reframing nature as balance-sheet risk. With land (Ulaanbaatar, August), biodiversity
(Yerevan, October) and climate (Antalya, November) COPs in one year, the WEF is explicitly framing exposure
through insurance costs, asset exposure, supply chains and workforce productivity, and notes a 30:1 private-
investment skew toward nature-negative over nature-positive activity. The CBD COP17 first review of the Global
Biodiversity Framework is the key near-term driver of nature-related disclosure expectations.
3. Heat is becoming a measured, not asserted, mortality risk. The ILO Colombia study, WHO's research
agenda on the climate–migration–health nexus, and WRI's wildfire-smoke mortality figures all move climate-and-
health from qualitative concern toward quantified exposure — the precondition for pricing it.
4. Divergence in the disclosure data supply. Convergence in Europe and internationally (EFRAG–PCAF, ISSB–
TNFD) is running alongside retrenchment in the United States (SEC rule rescission, G20 agenda). Actuaries should
expect the quality and comparability of climate risk inputs to become increasingly jurisdiction-dependent.
5. Risk transfer entering new markets. Verra's acceptance of insurance in place of buffer pools, ADB's use of
credit guarantees rather than index insurance for the same drought peril in Mongolia, and continuing sovereign
parametric activity all point to an expanding, and not always insurance-based, market for climate risk transfer.
NOTABLY QUIET
The Basel-based bodies were silent on climate throughout the fortnight: the BIS published no working paper after 22 July,
the BCBS nothing since 2 June, and the FSB's only August output concerned artificial intelligence and governance
appointments. The insurance-sector bodies were similarly in recess — the IAIS, Geneva Association, UNEP FI PSI and
FINZ all last published on climate in June or July, and the IAIS consultation page states that none is open. The WTO
published nothing on trade and environment after 7 July, and ISSA carried no climate item at all. This is a genuine
northern-hemisphere summer lull rather than a research gap; the pipeline restarts in mid-September with the OECD
Global Forum, the WTO Public Forum and the UNEP Global Sustainable Insurance Summit in Dublin, all in the same
week.
